4.1.3. Solver configuration
In the simulation experiment, the sequential decision approach used 
in practice (SEQU) and the approach for solving the MILP model 
utilizing Gurobi (M2G) proposed by Jiao et al. [28] in the online joint 
optimization of OA and PS (OJOOP) are compared with the proposed 
approach. We conduct small-scale instances for M1G, M2G, HADGS, 
and SEQU. M1G is a method that utilizes Gurobi to solve mathematical 
model M1, which is an exact method that serves as a benchmark for 
heuristic methods. Owing to their inability to solve large-scale instances 
within a finite time frame, both M1G and M2G are unsuitable for such 
scenarios. For example, even after running for 24 h at 𝑡 = 1  in a 
large-scale instance, M1G still leaves a gap of 15.4%. Consequently, we 
exclusively evaluated HADGS and SEQU in large-scale instances.
The relevant decision-making problems involved in RMFS at the 
operational level were defined in Section 1.1. Their corresponding 
decision-making approach used in the simulation experiments are pre-
sented in Table  7. The decision-making approach used in SEQU is a 
combination of Pod Match, Demand, Balanced, Nearest, WHCA∗
𝑛, Pod 
Batch, Emptiest, and Balanced. The choice of these aforementioned 
approaches in this study is based on the experimental findings reported 
by Merschformann et al. [7], because the combination of approaches ul-
timately achieves the highest throughput. See [7] for further details of 
such approaches, which are not repeated in this paper. The remaining 
decisions in OJOOPT and OJOOP are the same as SEQU except for the 
approaches for their respective joint decisions.
We set the threshold 𝜏 to 30 min and the value of 𝛼3 to 1000. In 
addition, the values of some important parameters in M1G and HADGS 
are determined by a series of preliminary experiments. The two weight 
coefficients of the optimization objectives are set to 𝛼1 = −40  and 
𝛼2 = 1 , respectively. We set the pod set quantity of evaluations to 
𝑙1 = 10 , and the quantity of local searches to 𝑙2 = 3 . We also set the 
threshold of available capacity of the workstation that triggers OJOOPT 
to 𝛿 = 1.
All results obtained by the model-based method (including M1G and 
M2G) in the experimental results are optimal solutions; that is, there is 
no optimal gap.
4.1.4. Performance metrics
This study employs the following four performance metrics to eval-
uate the relevant approaches:
• Throughput (𝑇 𝑃 ), which represents the operational efficiency of 
RMFS, is calculated by dividing the aggregate quantity of orders 
fulfilled by the hours spent fulfilling them.
• Robot distance (𝑅𝐷), which represents the aggregate distance 
traveled by all robots, is calculated by summing up the summation 
of distances traveled by each robot within the simulation horizon. 
A smaller 𝑅𝐷 indicates lower RMFS power consumption and 
running costs.
• Order distance (𝑂𝐷), which represents the average distance trav-
eled by robots per order, is calculated by dividing 𝑅𝐷 by the 
aggregate quantity of orders fulfilled. Similar to the 𝑅𝐷, the 
smaller the 𝑂𝐷, the less robot power consumption per order on 
average, and the lower the RMFS running cost.
• Pile-on (𝑃 𝑂), which represents the average quantity of orders 
fulfilled per pod movement, is calculated by dividing the aggre-
gate quantity of fulfilled orders by the aggregate quantity of pod 
movements. The larger the 𝑃 𝑂, the more orders are fulfilled per 
pod movement, which improves RMFS operational efficiency.
To mitigate the impact of the dynamic environment on RMFS, each 
instance is run ten times for a simulation horizon of 2 h.

4.3. Sensitivity analysis
4.3.1. Capacity threshold of workstations
As stated in Section 2.1, the 𝛿 parameter represents the threshold for 
the workstation available capacity. The solver is triggered when any 
workstation’s available capacity exceeds or equals 𝛿. This subsection 
explores the influence of the 𝛿 value on the performance metrics for 
small-scale instances. The larger the 𝛿, the greater the quantity of orders 
assigned in a single decision. This leads to a stronger positive impact on 
performance metrics owing to the increased correlation between these 
orders. However, a larger 𝛿 results in longer time intervals between 
consecutive decisions, potentially retracting some pods that can still 
fulfill orders in |𝑂| back to the storage zone, thereby adversely impact-
ing performance metrics. Therefore, the value of 𝛿 significantly impacts 
the performance of associated decision approaches. The influence of 
different 𝛿 values in small-scale instances (|𝑅| = 10 and |𝑂| = 100 ) is 
illustrated in Fig.  7.
As observed in Fig.  7, for all performance metrics, the performance 
of SEQU, HADGS, and M1G deteriorates with increasing 𝛿, whereas 
the performance of M2G initially improves and subsequently deterio-
rates. SEQU, HADGS, and M1G demonstrate peak performance when 
𝛿 = 1 , whereas M2G demonstrates peak performance when 𝛿 = 2 . 
There are several reasons for these aforementioned disparities. First, 
the correlation between orders is not considered in SEQU and thus 
cannot positively impact performance. Therefore, the greater the value 
of 𝛿, the greater the negative effect and the worse the performance of 
SEQU. Second, the correlation between orders is well considered in 
M2G, which can positively impact performance. Therefore, with the 
increase in 𝛿, the negative effect gradually outweighs the positive effect, 
resulting in an initial improvement and subsequent deterioration in 
M2G performance. Third, HADGS and M1G are joint decision-making 
methods that encompass OA, PS, and TA. These methods are effective 
only when the quantity of idle robots is not less than the quantity 
of pods that must be transported. Although HADGS and M1G also 
consider the correlation between orders, it can positively impact per-
formance. However, assigning more orders means that more pods must 
be transported, which may negatively impact performance. For HADGS 
and M1G, the increase in 𝛿 exhibits a greater negative impact on 
their performance than a positive impact, leading to a decline in their 
performance. In summary, in the simulation experiment, we set 𝛿 to 1 
for SEQU, HSDGS, and M1G, while 𝛿 is set to 2 for M2G.
4.3.2. Quantity of robots
The scale of the robot fleet deployed in RMFS, that is, the quantity 
of robots, significantly impacts RMFS operational efficiency. The ap-
propriate quantity of robots can enhance productivity, improve order

fulfillment speed, and reduce errors in picking and sorting operations. 
When the quantity of robots deployed in the RMFS is sufficient, all 
transportation tasks can be immediately executed by robots. This avoids 
the situation of “ picker waiting parts” insofar as possible. Therefore, 
increasing the quantity of robots deployed in RMFS can decrease the 
waiting time for pickers and enhance resource utilization, which pos-
itively impacts operational efficiency. Conversely, an increase in the 
quantity of robots results in longer queuing times in the queuing zone 
and amplifies the likelihood of congestion, collisions, and deadlocks 
along robots paths, thereby negatively impacting operational efficiency. 
Therefore, we conducted experiments to examine the impact of varying 
the quantity of robots on pertinent performance metrics.
Fig.  8 displays the influence of different quantities of robots in 
small-scale instances (|𝑅| = 6 , 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and |𝑂| = 100 ). The 𝑇 𝑃
of all four approaches demonstrates an upward trend as the quantity 
of robots increases, eventually stabilizing after reaching a threshold 
of ten robots. When the quantity of robots is small, there are more 
transportation tasks than available robots, leading to causes of “ picker 
waiting parts”. In this scenario, the positive impact of increasing the 
quantity of robots outweighs the negative impact, resulting in improved 
𝑇 𝑃 as the robot quantity increases. When the robot quantity reaches 
saturation, where there are consistently more available robots than 
transportation tasks, leading to causes of “ parts waiting picker”. In 
this scenario, the positive impact initially generated by increasing the 
quantity of robots is gradually counterbalanced by the negative impact, 
resulting in a plateau of 𝑇 𝑃 growth. For the 𝑅𝐷, an increase in robot 
quantity involved in transportation inevitably increases the aggregate 
distance traveled by all robots. Additionally, because the quantity of 
orders fulfilled in a transportation task is typically greater than 1 
(i.e., 𝑃 𝑂 > 1), the 𝑂𝐷 also increases with the increase in robot quantity. 
For the 𝑃 𝑂, as the quantity of robots increases, HADGS, M1G, and 
M2G all significantly increase in contrast to SEQU, which decreases. 
This discrepancy can be attributed to the fact that the increase in 𝑇 𝑃
for HADGS, M1G, and M2G is more pronounced compared to that for 
SEQU as shown in Fig.  8(a). Moreover, an increase in 𝑇 𝑃 is indicative 
of a higher quantity of orders that require fulfillment, while an increase 
in the quantity of robots implies a higher quantity of pod movements 
that can be provided. Therefore, per the definition of 𝑃 𝑂, when the 
increase in 𝑇 𝑃 exceeds that of the robots, 𝑃 𝑂 will increase, as observed 
in HADGS, M1G, and M2G. Conversely, when the increase in 𝑇 𝑃 is less 
than that of the robots, 𝑃 𝑂 will decrease, as observed in SEQU.
Fig.  9 shows the influence of different quantities of robots in large-
scale instances (|𝑅| = 60, 70, 80, 90, 100, 110, 120, and |𝑂| = 500). As the 
quantity of robots increases, the changing trend of SEQU and HADGS 
in large-scale instances remains consistent with that in small-scale 
instances across the four performance metrics. The reasons for these 
changes are also the same as those observed in small-scale instances. 
The only disparity is that, as the quantity of robots increases, the 𝑃 𝑂
of HADGS exhibits an upward trend in large-scale instances, albeit a 
downward trend in small-scale instances. The reason for this disparity 
is that in large-scale instances, the increase in the quantity of robots 
surpasses the corresponding increase in 𝑇 𝑃 for HADGS. Consequently, 
the 𝑃 𝑂 of HADGS decreases with the increasing robot quantity.
The aforementioned results demonstrate that, by optimizing rele-
vant decision-making approaches, the quantity of robots deployed in 
RMFS could be effectively decreased. This suggests that enhancing 
the performance of the decision-making approach can lead to more 
optimal resource allocation and utilization, resulting in a reduction of 
the necessity for numerous robots. Specifically, in small-scale instances, 
the 𝑇 𝑃 of M1G when |𝑅| = 6  remains 8% higher than that of SEQU 
when |𝑅| = 12 while reducing the corresponding 𝑅𝐷 by 38%; in large-
scale instances, the 𝑇 𝑃 of HADGS when |𝑅| = 60 remains 6% higher 
than that of SEQU when |𝑅| = 120 while reducing the corresponding 
𝑅𝐷 by 50%. Hence, M1G and HADGS employ at most half the quantity 
of robots employed in SEQU to achieve an equivalent throughput for 
both small and large instances. Meanwhile, the corresponding distance 
traveled by robots is only 62 and 57% of that in SEQU, respectively.

4.4. Managerial insights
Based on the results of these simulation experiments, the following 
warehouse managerial insights were identified:
• Reducing the number of pod movements does not necessarily en-
hance warehouse operational efficiency, which deviates from con-
ventional perspectives in existing literature. For example, from 
the perspective of system operational costs, Teck and Dewil [26] 
found that only minimizing the number of pod movements is 
insufficient and using distance as the optimization objective is 
more suitable for optimizing operational costs. From the perspec-
tive of system operational efficiency (i.e. throughput), this study 
found no correlation between the number of pod movements and 
system operational efficiency, that is, a reduction in the number

5. Conclusion
This study investigated the online joint optimization for OA, PS, and 
TA in RMFS during the order picking process. To enhance decision-
making efficiency, the study accounted for the impact of current de-
cisions on future decisions. A dynamic system model for RMFS was 
established, alongside the development of a novel MILP model to 
address critical challenges. To solve large-scale instances effectively, a 
customized HADGS was devised.
The proposed model and approach were rigorously evaluated using 
small- and large-scale instances within established dynamic systems.
